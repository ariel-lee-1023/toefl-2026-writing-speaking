# TOEFL Writing Templates: Write an Email — Magoosh
**Format**: md (from pdf) | **Pages**: ~19 | **Sections**: skeleton + 5 task types + practice method | **Depth**: study
**Status**: third-party. Where it conflicts with `reference-ets-task-specs.md`, ETS wins. Its unique value is (a) the **7-minute clock**, which ETS never states, and (b) a taxonomy of **speech acts** the ETS rubric scores under "formulation of actions such as requests, refusals, criticisms."

## Mental Model (read first)
Magoosh's own framing: templates are **scaffolding, not a script** — they supply *how to say it*, never *what to say*. The guide is unusually candid that its own phrases are a liability at the top: "if thousands of students are reading this same guide, and everyone always uses [the same line], those emails start to sound alike," and the score-5 rubric demands *idiomatic* word choice, which "means language that sounds like you, not like a template." **Read this file as a map of the required speech acts, then generate your own realizations of them.** The task-type taxonomy is durable; the specific phrases are disposable and should be treated as such.

## Timing and length (Magoosh's numbers, not ETS's)
- **7 minutes total**: ~1 min read + plan · 4–5 min write · 1–2 min proofread.
- **Target 100–120 words**, **~2 sentences per bullet**, one short paragraph per bullet.
- **ETS check**: ETS gives no word target and says a *concise* message scores well if complete, relevant, and organized. Treat 100–120 as a practical floor-and-ceiling for hitting "adequate elaboration" in 7 minutes, not as a rule.

## Frameworks & Structure

### The Universal Email Skeleton
Greeting → **opening sentence** (1 line of context; optional) → **3 body paragraphs, one per prompt bullet** → closing sentence → sign-off + name.
- **Register ladder** (match sign-off to greeting; most prompts want the middle of the ladder — everyday email English, not an academic paper, not a text):
  | Recipient | Greeting | Sign-off |
  |---|---|---|
  | Friend / peer | *Hi [First]!* | Best, / Take care, / Thanks, |
  | Semi-formal (club leader, coworker, neighbor) | *Hi / Hello / Dear [First],* | Best regards, / Thank you, / All the best, |
  | Authority (professor, manager, director) | *Dear [Title] [Last],* | Sincerely, / Kind regards, / Respectfully, |
  | Role-based (editor, coordinator, committee) | *Dear [Role],* / *Hello,* | Sincerely, |
- **Budget rule**: greeting, opening, closing, and sign-off "matter for register but don't really earn you points." Spend the time on bodies. ETS agrees implicitly — social conventions are criterion 3, elaboration is criterion 1.

### The five task types (the actually transferable content)
Each is a **speech act with a three-move bullet structure**. Recognize the type from the prompt's bullets in ~5 seconds, then execute the moves.

1. **Requesting Help or Action** — bullets: explain situation → describe what you need → make the request.
   - **How**: every request must be **interrogative or conditional** — *Would you be available to…? / I was wondering if you could… / Would it work for you to…?* **Anti-pattern**: bare imperatives ("Help me with this", "Meet me Friday") read as demanding in English and cost you on social conventions.
   - **Register tell**: "I was wondering if you could…" is the softest and belongs with authority figures; "Would it work for you to…?" is peer-level.
2. **Giving Feedback and Suggestions** — bullets: describe the experience → identify the issue → suggest an improvement.
   - **How**: **describe the problem, don't judge it.** The guide's own contrast: *"The sound quality was terrible"* vs *"I did notice that the sound quality could have been better."* Same information, and only the second is scored as appropriate social conventions.
   - **Structure**: lead with a genuine positive, pivot on *That said,* / *However, I did notice that…*, then position the fix as easy ("A small change that could really help is…").
3. **Offering Advice or Perspective** — bullets: acknowledge their situation → share your perspective (weighing options) → suggest an alternative or next step.
   - **How**: validate before advising ("I can see why you're having a hard time deciding"), then commit to a recommendation. This is the type where **invented personal experience pays most** — see Decision Rules.
4. **Recruiting, Inviting, or Recommending** — bullets: explain the initiative → say what's needed / why *they* fit → invite them.
   - **How**: **frame the ask as an opportunity, not a chore.** Guide's contrast: "We need someone to set up tables" vs "We're looking for someone to help with setup — it's a great way to be involved from the start." The personal-appeal move ("The reason I thought of you is that…") does double duty as elaboration.
5. **Navigating Disagreement or Constraints** — bullets: acknowledge their position → explain the constraint → propose a compromise.
   - **How**: **lead with the fact, not the apology.** "Unfortunately, the schedule has already been set" beats a sprawling apology that reads as excuse-making. Be honest, be brief, move to the solution.
   - This is the type that most directly exercises the C1 descriptor **"express strong disagreement diplomatically."**

- **Types mix.** Real prompts blend them — feedback plus a request, advice plus an invitation. Match the move to what each bullet actually asks, not to a category label.
- **Anti-pattern — one register throughout regardless of recipient**: a formal phrase in a peer email reads stiff; a casual one to a professor reads careless. Both lose points on the explicitly scored social-conventions criterion.

### Transitions by function
Requests: *With that in mind, / That's why I'm reaching out to ask if / Given the situation,* · Feedback: *That said, / On the other hand, / Even so,* · Advice: *Then again, there's another side to consider. / Regardless of what you choose,* · Recruiting: *Here's where I could use your help. / I immediately thought of you because* · Disagreement: *That said, I do want to find a solution. / I know this isn't what you were hoping to hear, but*

## Worked Example — Type 2, and what each move is doing
*(Prompt: a coworker organized a team lunch; give feedback and suggest an idea for next time. Reconstructed from the guide's specimen, ~120 words, semi-formal throughout.)*

**Bullet 1 (experience)** — names the event and one specific thing that worked: the venue's atmosphere, and seeing colleagues outside the office. **Bullet 2 (issue)** — pivots on *That said*, and reports rather than judges: the seating meant people only talked to the two or three nearest them, "which felt like a missed opportunity." Note the issue is **concrete and consequential**, not an adjective. **Bullet 3 (suggestion)** — offers two options (a round table, or splitting into smaller groups) and states the benefit ("everyone gets a chance to mix"). **Close** — thanks them and acknowledges the effort involved.

Every structural phrase came from a menu; **every fact — the restaurant, the seating, the round table — was invented.** That split is the whole method: borrowed frame, original substance. And it's exactly the split ETS penalizes in the other direction — if the *substance* were borrowed too, the response would score as memorized.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- **Invented details are not merely allowed, they're the point.** The guide: details "don't need to be true — they need to be **realistic**." A named dish, a specific day, a plausible number. Generic elaboration is the most common cause of a 3 instead of a 5.
- **Reconciling templates with ETS's memorization penalty** (the central judgment call in this whole library): ETS penalizes **memorized content and formulaic framing** — invented statistics, prefabricated intros/conclusions, borrowed argumentation. It does *not* penalize ordinary email conventions; "I hope you're doing well" is what real emails contain. **Safe**: conventional greetings, sign-offs, and softeners. **Unsafe**: any sentence that would survive unchanged into a different prompt. Apply that test to every sentence you didn't invent.
- **Practice protocol — three drafts per prompt** (this is the part of the guide worth keeping): draft 1 with the menus open, deliberately *choosing*; draft 2 on the same prompt with **entirely different phrases**; draft 3 from memory with the guide closed. Five prompts, one per task type = 15 emails. The point of draft 2 is to break the one-phrase-per-slot habit that makes writing rigid — the B2 marker.
- **When a bullet is thin, add a consequence, not an adjective.** "The seating was bad" → "we only talked to the same two or three people all evening."
- **When you finish under 100 words with time left**, elaborate the *weakest* bullet, then proofread. Do not add a conclusion — ETS names padded conclusions as a penalized pattern.
- **Never spend more than a few seconds on the greeting.** Pick, type, move.

## Key Takeaways
1. **7 minutes: 1 plan / 4–5 write / 1–2 proofread. ~100–120 words, ~2 sentences per bullet.**
2. **The bullets are your outline** — one short paragraph each, in order.
3. **Five task types**, identified from the bullets in seconds: request · feedback · advice · recruit/invite · decline/negotiate.
4. **Requests take question or conditional form; criticism describes rather than judges; refusals lead with the fact, not the apology.** These are exactly what ETS scores as "formulation of actions such as requests, refusals, criticisms."
5. **Match register to recipient and keep it consistent** across greeting, body, and sign-off.
6. **Borrow the frame, invent the substance** — realistic, specific, prompt-anchored.
7. **Templates get you to a competent 4; they cannot deliver the 5**, which needs elaboration, syntactic variety, and word choice that sounds like you. Use the menus to learn the patterns, then retire them.
