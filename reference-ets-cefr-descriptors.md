# Writing & Speaking Section Performance Descriptors (Appendix: Performance Feedback for Test Takers) — ETS
**Format**: md (from pdf) | **Pages**: ~10 (pp. 351–360) | **Sections**: 2 scales × 6 bands | **Depth**: study
**Role in this library**: this file defines **the target**. `reference-ets-task-specs.md` defines the *tasks*; this one defines what a C2 performance *is*.

## Mental Model (read first)
Two different scales are in play and conflating them is the most common analytical error:
- **Section scores run 1–6 in half-point steps** and map onto CEFR: **6 = C2**, 5–5.5 = C1, 4–4.5 = B2, 3–3.5 = B1, 2–2.5 = A2, 1–1.5 = A1.
- **Individual task scoring guides run 0–5** (see the task-specs file). A "5" on an email is *not* a section 6.
The descriptors are **CEFR can-do statements, not rubric criteria** — they describe capability, so they tell you what to *build*, while the rubrics tell you what gets *credited*. The gap between C1 and C2 in both skills is not accuracy (C1 is already accurate) but **precision, ambiguity elimination, and the absence of any felt restriction on what you can say**.

## Writing — Section Score 6 (CEFR C2)
> Can produce a broad range of extended and complex texts, including on abstract, nuanced, or highly technical topics, featuring sophisticated vocabulary, intricate sentence structures, and **subtle distinctions of style or meaning**.

Three can-do statements — these are the operational definition of C2 writing:
1. **Comprehensive and unrestricted mastery** of a wide range of language, used to (a) **formulate thoughts precisely**, (b) **give emphasis**, and (c) **eliminate ambiguity**.
2. **Convey finer shades of meaning** with reasonable accuracy via a wide range of **qualifying devices** — the descriptor names two classes: **adverbs showing degree** (*marginally, decisively, only nominally, disproportionately*) and **clauses showing limitations** (*insofar as…, provided that…, except where…, to the extent that…*).
3. **Strong command of a broad lexical repertoire including idiomatic expressions**, plus **awareness of connotative levels of meaning** (that *thrifty / frugal / stingy* denote near-identically and evaluate oppositely).

- **What C2 is NOT**: nothing in the descriptor rewards length, elevated diction, or complex-for-its-own-sake syntax. "Intricate structures" appears alongside "eliminate ambiguity" — the intricacy exists *to be more exact*, not to look harder.
- **ETS's advice at this level** (they concede you're already an expert writer): analyze the structure and rhetorical strategies of exemplary academic writing; **exchange peer feedback in both directions** — critiquing others sharpens your own editing and revision; read actively in your discipline. "Expert writers also tend to be enthusiastic readers."

## Writing — Section Score 5–5.5 (CEFR C1)
Writes on a wide range of academic and nonacademic topics **with confidence and clarity**. Can:
- use a **broad range of complex grammatical structures appropriately and with flexibility**;
- establish **consistent and helpful layout, paragraphing, and punctuation**;
- **precisely qualify opinions in relation to degrees of certainty and uncertainty**;
- **express strong disagreement diplomatically.**

**Diagnostic value**: the last two are the C1/C2 boundary markers most visible in TOEFL-length writing. If you can hedge with calibration (*almost certainly / on balance / in most but not all cases*) and disagree hard without discourtesy, you are at least C1. C2 adds doing this **without any sign of restricting what you wanted to say** — no idea abandoned because the English was out of reach.

## Writing — Section Score 4–4.5 (CEFR B2) *(the floor you must clear)*
Well-organized and coherent text with a range of linking devices; good command of simple structures and **some complex forms, but used rigidly and with some inaccuracy**; can develop a clear description or narrative with relevant supporting detail; **lexical accuracy generally high, occasional wrong word choice doesn't hinder communication.**
- **The B2→C1 tell is rigidity**, not error count: complex structures deployed as fixed templates rather than flexibly. This is precisely the failure mode that template over-reliance produces — see the Magoosh reference files.

*(Lower bands, for completeness: **3–3.5 / B1** — basic factual emails, wide range of *simple* language, main point comprehensible, major errors on complex thoughts. **2–2.5 / A2** — brief everyday expressions, basic mistakes, intent usually clear. **1–1.5 / A1** — isolated words, phrases, short sentences.)*

## Speaking — Section Score 6 (CEFR C2)
> Communicates fluently, precisely, and effectively on a wide range of complex and abstract topics, **adapting language with ease to suit different academic and professional contexts**; sophisticated vocabulary, complex structures, and **subtle distinctions of style, tone, and meaning**.

Three can-do statements:
1. **Express themselves at length with a natural, effortless, smooth flow, pausing only to reflect on precisely the right word or to find an appropriate example.** — Note what this legitimizes: **pausing is C2 behavior when it is a search for precision.** It is *not* the same as hesitation from lexical shortage, and raters distinguish them by what follows the pause.
2. **Full and reliable mastery** of a broad range of language to formulate thoughts precisely, **give emphasis and differentiate**, and eliminate ambiguity, **with no signs of having to restrict what they want to say.**
3. Provide **clear, smoothly flowing, elaborate, and often memorable descriptions.**

- **ETS's advice at this level**: record your own performances and critically evaluate fluency, coherence, and overall effectiveness; study recordings of expert speakers for how they shift **tone and register** across seminar / conference / interview contexts; target individual key words or phrases you mispronounce.

## Speaking — Section Score 5–5.5 (CEFR C1)
- Expresses themselves **fluently and spontaneously, almost effortlessly** — "only a difficult subject can hinder a smooth flow of language";
- uses a **broad range of complex grammatical structures and less common vocabulary appropriately**;
- uses **the full range of phonological features in English with sufficient control to ensure intelligibility**.

**Diagnostic value**: C1 phonology is defined by *sufficiency for intelligibility*, not native-likeness. Consistent with the task-specs chapter: accent never costs points unless it impedes understanding. The C1→C2 move is not accent reduction — it is **eliminating the topic-dependence of your fluency**.

## Speaking — Section Score 4–4.5 (CEFR B2) *(the floor)*
Even tempo but **hesitant while searching for patterns and expressions**, few noticeably long pauses; fairly high grammatical control, no misunderstanding-causing errors, intelligible throughout **despite a few systematic mispronunciations**; can explain a viewpoint giving pros and cons; can develop a clear description or narrative with relevant detail and examples.
- **"Systematic mispronunciations"** is the operative phrase: B2 speakers mispronounce the *same* sounds repeatedly. Fixing a small set of recurring segmental errors is the highest-leverage B2→C1 delivery move.

*(Lower bands: **3–3.5 / B1** — comprehensible but pausing for grammatical/lexical planning and repair is very evident in longer stretches; brief explanations for opinions; copes via circumlocution. **2–2.5 / A2** — short exchanges, noticeable hesitation and false starts, simple direct comparisons. **1–1.5 / A1** — isolated, mainly prepackaged utterances with much pausing.)*

## Cross-band practice methods ETS actually prescribes
Ranked here by leverage for a C1 candidate pushing to C2, drawn from the C1/B2 advice sections:
- **Record → transcribe → re-say.** Read a short article, record yourself summarizing it *and reacting to it*, transcribe the recording, then rewrite each sentence a different way. This is the only exercise in the appendix that attacks **range** and **self-monitoring** simultaneously.
- **Shadowing** — repeat aloud after a podcast/audio speaker, imitating pronunciation, intonation, and rhythm. Directly trains *Listen and Repeat* retention and the prosody half of Delivery.
- **Read opinion writing analytically**: identify the writer's opinion, then notice **how they handle possible objections to it.** This is the mechanism behind "express strong disagreement diplomatically."
- **Write across genres deliberately** — emails, messages, short essays, online posts, on both academic and everyday topics — because the 2026 Writing section spans exactly that range.
- **Edit others' writing**, not only your own.
- **Anti-pattern** — practicing only the task you're weakest at, in isolation. The descriptors are *section*-level: the Writing score aggregates Build a Sentence, Email, and Discussion.

## Worked Example — turning one C1 sentence into a C2 sentence
Take a defensible C1 discussion sentence: *"I disagree with Jordan because gig work is unstable and workers have no benefits."* It is accurate, clear, and correctly qualifies nothing.

Apply the three C2 writing can-dos in order:
1. **Eliminate ambiguity** — *unstable* conflates two different claims (income variance vs. contract termination risk). Split them: unpredictable weekly earnings, and the absence of any notice period.
2. **Add qualifying devices** — a degree adverb and a limiting clause: *"…is unpredictable **week to week**, at least **where platforms allocate work algorithmically**…"* The limitation is not hedging; it makes the claim falsifiable and therefore stronger.
3. **Use connotation deliberately** — *"workers have no benefits"* → *"workers **absorb** risks that an employer would otherwise **carry**."* Same denotation; *absorb/carry* imports the allocation-of-burden frame that the sentence is actually arguing.

Result: *"Jordan is right that the benefits gap matters, but I'd locate the harm more precisely: the real cost isn't the missing insurance so much as the fact that earnings vary week to week, at least where platforms allocate work algorithmically — which means workers absorb a planning risk that an employer would ordinarily carry."*
Longer by design? No — **denser**. Every added word does one of the three jobs. That is the C2 signature.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- **When choosing between a rarer word and a more exact word, take the exact one.** "Sophisticated vocabulary" is listed *after* "formulate thoughts precisely" in the C2 descriptor, and precision is what it serves.
- **When you feel a pause coming in speaking**: if you're pausing to find *the right* word or *an example*, that's C2-legal — let it land and then deliver something precise. If you're pausing because the word isn't there, **reroute to a simpler formulation immediately** rather than stalling; a filled 45 seconds of clear B2+ speech outscores a choppy attempt at C1 vocabulary.
- **When you want to disagree strongly**: concede the strongest version of their point *first*, then locate the disagreement narrowly. Diplomatic disagreement is an explicit C1 can-do and near-free points.
- **When you catch yourself abandoning an idea because the English is hard**, that abandonment *is* the C1 ceiling. Note the idea, and afterwards drill the structure you lacked — that list is your personal C2 syllabus.
- **When self-assessing a practice response**, score capability (these descriptors) and credit (the 0–5 rubrics) separately. They fail differently: capability gaps show as rigidity and restriction; credit gaps show as thin elaboration and unaddressed prompt actions.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Section scores 1–6 map to CEFR A1–C2; task rubrics run 0–5.** Never conflate them.
2. **C2 = precision + emphasis + ambiguity elimination**, executed with **no sign of restricting what you want to say**.
3. The named C2 lexical tools are **degree adverbs, limiting clauses, idiom, and connotative awareness** — qualification, not decoration.
4. **C1 speaking fluency is topic-dependent; C2 fluency is not.** That is the actual gap.
5. **Pausing to find the precise word is C2 behavior**; pausing from lexical shortage is B1/B2 behavior. Same silence, opposite diagnosis.
6. **Rigidity, not error rate, marks B2** — templates used as fixed frames keep you there.
7. Highest-leverage drills: **record→transcribe→re-say**, **shadowing**, and **analyzing how published writers handle objections**.
