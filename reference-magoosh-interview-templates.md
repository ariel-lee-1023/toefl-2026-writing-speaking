# TOEFL Speaking Templates: Interview Task — Magoosh

**Status**: third-party. Where it conflicts with `reference-ets-task-specs.md`, ETS wins. Unique value: a **time-allocated response shape** for the 45-second window, which ETS describes only as "use the full time."

## Mental Model (read first)
The binding constraint is **zero preparation time**. The moment the interviewer stops speaking, you start — so any second spent deciding *how to begin* is a second permanently lost from elaboration. The skeleton exists to make the opening reflexive so your working memory goes entirely to *what to say*. The guide is explicit about the tradeoff it creates: reuse "Honestly, I'd say…" on all four questions and you "run the risk of sounding robotic and too rehearsed" — which collides directly with ETS's rule that memorized-sounding language scores lower. **Learn the shape; rotate the phrasing.**

## Output format — HARD CONSTRAINT
This task is **spoken**, so the risk isn't in your delivery — it's in how a sample answer gets *written down* during practice or coaching. A model answer must be transcribed as **continuous spoken prose**: no bullets, no headings, no bold labels, no `Slot 1:` annotations inside the answer itself. The C–D–E–F slots are a planning device, never a visible structure. Writing a practice answer as a bulleted list trains exactly the wrong thing, because ETS's score-4 speaking band is elaborated content that **"may lack effective sentence-level connectors"** — the connectives *are* the difference between a 4 and a 5, and a bulleted draft has none to practice.

## The Universal Response Skeleton — C–D–E–F (45 seconds, ~110 words)
| Slot | ~Time | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **C — Commit** | ~8 s | State your position outright. Don't hedge, don't list options, **don't repeat the question.** If the listener doesn't know your stance from the start, everything after is harder to follow. |
| **D — Detail** | ~15 s | Your main reason. Why do you feel that way? Elaboration starts here, and **elaboration is the rubric's top content criterion.** |
| **E — Elaborate** | ~15 s | A second detail, an example, or more depth on the first reason. **A specific example is usually stronger than an abstract reason.** |
| **F — Finish** | ~7 s | **Optional.** A brief close, *or* keep elaborating until time runs out. |

- **The time splits are guides, not stopwatches.** If Commit takes three seconds and the example deserves twenty-five, run with it. What matters is **moving from slot to slot instead of getting stuck on one.**
- **~110 words fills 45 seconds at a conversational pace** — a useful practice target, not a delivery goal.
- **Anti-pattern — treating Finish as required.** The rubric rewards on-topic elaboration, not "conclusions." With 6–7 seconds left and something real still to say, **keep talking.** Burning the tail on a wrap-up phrase you didn't need is a straight loss.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Menu 1 — Commit
*Core*: *Honestly, I'd say… / For me, it'd be… / In my opinion, I believe that… / I'd go with…*
*Variety*: *If I had to choose, I'd pick… / Well, the way I see it… / I tend to think that… / I'd probably lean toward…*
- **Either-or and debate questions**: **commit to one side.** The rubric rewards clarity and elaboration, **not balance**. A focused one-sided answer beats a "both have their points" answer that runs out of time. You may nod to the other side in one clause ("I can see why people like Y, but…") before committing.

### Menu 2 — First detail
*Core*: *The biggest reason is that… / Part of it is that… / What I like about it is… / It mostly comes down to…*
*Variety*: *A lot of it has to do with… / One thing that stands out is… / I think it's mainly because… / What makes a difference for me is…*
- **Rule**: **one reason in this slot, fully explained.** Packing two reasons into one sentence makes both shallow. Save the second for E.

### Menu 3 — Elaborate
*Core*: *To add to that… / On top of that… / Another thing is… / For example… / Just to give you an example…*
*Variety*: *I've noticed that… / Beyond that… / Just last week, actually… / A good example would be…*
- **This is where invented examples do the most work.** "Just last week, I…" or "This one time, my friend…" makes an answer specific instantly and gives you a scene to describe — which is what fills the remaining seconds without padding.

### Menu 4 — Finish (optional)
*So that's my take. / That's basically my view. / So overall, that's how I feel. / Anyway, those are the main reasons. / At least, that's how it works for me. / In the end, that's what I like best.* — or restate the position: *And that's why I believe X is best.*

### Menu 5 — Transitions and flow (the range signal)
Grouped by function; **two or three across a 45-second response is plenty**, best placed *between sentences inside D and E* where the slot phrases have already done their structural work.
- **Opinion**: *If you ask me, / From my point of view, / Personally,*
- **Listing**: *First of all, / For one thing, / Second,*
- **Adding**: *Furthermore, / Plus, / What's more,* — smoother than listing when you're stacking support
- **Cause/effect**: *That's because… / As a result, / Because of that,*
- **Contrasting**: *Unlike…, / On the other hand, / In contrast,*
- **Similarity**: *Likewise, / Similarly, / In the same way,*
- **Conceding / dismissing**: *Granted, / That said, / Regardless,*
- **Examples**: *For instance, / Take …, for example. / A case in point is…*
- **Clarifying / emphasizing**: *What I mean is… / In other words, / In fact,*
- **Wrapping up**: *Overall, / In short, / All in all,*

## Worked Example — a debate question, slot by slot
*(Question: some say it's better to take photos quickly and naturally, others say plan each photo carefully — which do you agree with, and why? Reconstructed, ~110 words.)*

**C**: *Honestly, I'd say* — leans to quick and natural. One clause, no hedging. **D**: *The biggest reason is that* the best moments last only seconds, so planning means the moment is already gone — then immediately concrete: *For instance,* a nephew who won't smile if he knows a photo is being taken, but who can be caught grinning at random. **E**: *On top of that* — the photos the speaker loves most were never the set-up ones; they're the unplanned ones, like friends goofing around in a car; *on the other hand,* planned photos feel stiff. **F**: *So that's my take* — quick and natural usually wins.

Three things to notice: every structural phrase came from a menu while **every detail (the nephew, the car) is invented**; the register is spoken, not essayistic ("kind of stiff", "usually wins"), which is what the Interview task asks for; and **E adds a contrast rather than a second parallel reason** — that's what makes the answer feel like thinking rather than listing. It matches the shape ETS's own four score-5 samples share: **stance + concrete personal example + a contrast**.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- **When you see both sides**: pick whichever is **easier to explain in English**, not whichever you believe. Arguing both in 45 seconds produces two underdeveloped halves and a confused listener.
- **When a question doesn't match any prepared phrase**: fall back on the four most universal — *Honestly, I'd say… / The biggest reason is that… / For example… / So that's my take.* C–D–E–F covers any Interview question.
- **When you have 6–7 seconds left and material remaining**: **keep elaborating; skip F.** When you're out of material, use F to exit cleanly rather than trailing off.
- **Rotate across the four-question set.** Aim for a different Commit opener on each of the four; same for Detail and Finish. The guide's own test is not "do I know one good opener" but "can I cycle through several."
- **Reconciling menus with ETS's memorization penalty**: connectives and stance markers are how fluent speakers actually talk, and ETS never penalizes them — its own score-5 samples are full of *I think it depends*, *for example*, *but*, *so, yeah*. What ETS penalizes is **memorized sentences and prefabricated openers/closers used to lengthen a response.** Safe: a stance marker plus your own content. Unsafe: any full sentence you could have said before hearing the question.
- **Delivery is half the score and templates can't touch it.** The guide is explicit that pace, pronunciation, and intonation need separate work — shadowing. A phrase bank cannot fix a choppy 3.
- **Record every practice answer.** Listening back is the fastest way to catch the specific habits that cap the score: long pauses, repeated phrases, rushed pace, unclear pronunciation. "Most students who plateau are stuck because they're not hearing what their listener hears."
- **Practice protocol**: pick **three favorite phrases per slot (twelve total)** — not the full menu, which causes hesitation under time pressure. Then: answer with the menu open and *choosing* → answer a **full four-question set** rotating openers → answer the same set with the guide closed. Target ≥20 timed answers before test day.

## Key Takeaways
1. **45 seconds, no prep. C–D–E–F: Commit ~8s, Detail ~15s, Elaborate ~15s, Finish ~7s (optional). ~110 words.**
2. **Commit immediately** — no hedging, no listing options, and never repeat the question back.
3. **One reason in D, fully explained.** The second reason belongs in E.
4. **A specific, invented, plausible example beats an abstract reason** — and gives you material to fill the clock.
5. **Commit to one side on either-or and debate questions.** Balance is not rewarded; clarity and elaboration are.
6. **Finish is optional** — elaborating to the buzzer outscores a wrap-up phrase.
7. **Two or three transitions per answer**, placed inside D and E.
8. **Rotate phrasing across the four questions**, and work delivery separately by shadowing and recording — the templates cannot reach it.
