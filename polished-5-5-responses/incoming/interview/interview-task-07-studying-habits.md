## Title
Studying Habits

## Q1 Prompt
Scenario: You have agreed to participate in a research study about studying habits. You will have a short online interview with a researcher. The researcher will ask you some questions.

Thank you for taking the time to speak with me. I'd like to ask you some questions about your studying habits. First, do you prefer studying alone or with a group? How often do you study?

## Q2 Prompt
I see. If you were helping someone prepare for a test, what study methods would you recommend? For example, would you suggest using practice tests, taking notes, or something else?

## Q3 Prompt
Interesting. Next, I'd like to get your opinion. In recent years, many people have started using digital tools and online apps to study. Do you think these methods should be used in schools, or only for personal study? Explain your answer.

## Q4 Prompt
Good points. I just have one more question. Some people believe that having good study habits is essential for academic success. Do you agree with this idea? Or do you think there are other factors that contribute to academic success? Explain why you think so.

## Q1 My Polished Response
I prefer studying alone, and I usually study for about an hour on most weekdays. Before an exam, I add a longer session at the weekend. Working alone lets me choose my own pace and spend extra time on anything I haven't understood. For example, when I'm reading a difficult legal case, I can pause and work through the reasoning without holding anyone else up. I also put my phone away so I don't keep interrupting myself. After I've done that individual work, I sometimes discuss a difficult point with a friend to check my understanding.

## Q2 My Polished Response
I'd recommend using practice questions and explaining the answers in your own words. Reading notes can make a topic feel familiar, but that doesn't always mean you can use the information. For example, after reviewing a chapter, I'd close the book and try a few questions. If I got one wrong, I'd explain what confused me and work through a similar problem. I would also ask a friend to challenge my explanation. If I couldn't make it clear, I'd know what to review next. That gives me a much better plan than simply reading the whole chapter again.

## Q3 My Polished Response
I think digital study tools belong in schools as well as in personal study, provided they help students think for themselves. For example, a short online quiz can show a teacher which idea the class hasn't understood. The teacher can then ask students to explain their answers and discuss why another option is wrong. That turns the tool into a starting point for learning. However, an app that simply supplies finished answers could let students skip the thinking. I'd judge the tool by what students do with it, rather than assuming that using a screen automatically improves a lesson.

## Q4 My Polished Response
I agree that good study habits are essential, but they aren't enough on their own. A regular routine helps students make progress instead of leaving everything until the night before an exam. However, the quality of teaching and access to useful feedback matter too. A student might study every evening but keep making the same mistake because nobody explains where the reasoning goes wrong. Once a teacher identifies that gap, the student's effort becomes much more effective. So I'd combine consistent independent work with asking questions and getting feedback, rather than measuring progress only by the hours spent.
