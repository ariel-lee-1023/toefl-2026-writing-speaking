# Urban Planning Gentrification

## Prompt
Your professor is teaching a class on urban planning. Write a post responding to the professor's question.
In your response, you should do the following.

Express and support your opinion.
Make a contribution to the discussion in your own words.
An effective response will contain at least 100 words.

Dr. Okafor
We've been discussing the effects of gentrification on urban neighborhoods. Gentrification occurs when wealthier residents and businesses move into lower-income areas, often leading to rising property values and changes in the character of the neighborhood. Some argue that gentrification revitalizes struggling communities by bringing new investment and improved services. Others contend that it displaces long-time residents who can no longer afford to live there. What is your perspective?

Lena
I think gentrification can be positive for neighborhoods. New businesses, better infrastructure, and improved safety attract more people and create opportunities. When investment flows into an area, it can raise the overall quality of life for everyone, including current residents who benefit from upgraded schools and public spaces.

Jordan
I believe gentrification is harmful because it pushes out the very people who built the community in the first place. As rents and property taxes rise, longtime residents and small business owners are forced to leave. The neighborhood may look better on the surface, but its original identity and culture are often lost.

## My Polished Response
While Lena rightly points out that initial private investments can modernize local infrastructure and update public spaces, what this argument overlooks is that such upgrades rarely serve the original population when unmitigated by policy. As capital flows into a neighborhood, escalating property tax reassessments and commercial rent spikes almost immediately price out long-standing residents and independent business owners—the very groups who sustained the area during periods of disinvestment. I agree with Jordan that this dynamic ultimately dismantles the social fabric and cultural identity that defined the community for decades. First, fixed-income homeowners often absorb tax burdens aligned with speculative market values rather than their actual household income; second, small businesses operating on narrow margins face lease renewals they simply cannot absorb. Lena is correct that infrastructure improvements are inherently desirable, but Jordan’s perspective is more compelling because physical revitalization offers little value to residents who are displaced before they can enjoy it.

## My Draft
I agree with Jordan that gentrification generally has a negative impact on communities, although it can create new business opportunities for some groups of people, as Lena pointed out. While Lena rightly notes that initial investments can bring updated infrastructure and cleaner public spaces, what this argument overlooks is that these surface-level upgrades rarely benefit the original residents, who are almost immediately priced out by soaring property taxes and skyrocketing rents. When long-term residents are forced to relocate, the social fabric and cultural heritage that defined the neighborhood for decades are permanently dismantled.

## My Diagnosis
The original draft establishes a clear stance and successfully addresses both student perspectives, but it stops short of Score 5 due to abstract elaboration and limited syntactic range. While claims regarding displacement and rising costs are relevant, the draft lacks a concrete mechanism detailing how economic pressures operate on specific groups (e.g., fixed-income owners or small businesses). Additionally, the sentence structure relies on straightforward concession clauses ("While Lena rightly notes...") without deploying higher-level qualifying devices or degree adverbs that eliminate ambiguity and demonstrate C2-level precision.

## My What Changed & Why
To elevate the draft to a Score 5, elaboration was deepened by introducing specific economic mechanisms—namely, property tax reassessments driven by speculative market values and unmanageable commercial lease renewals for small businesses. Syntactic control was enhanced through calibrated qualifications ("when unmitigated by policy," "disproportionately") that refine the scope of the claims. The response was further structured in continuous prose using explicit sequencing connectives ("First... second...") to articulate parallel supports, and closed by directly re-weighing both students by name ("Lena is correct that... but Jordan's perspective is more compelling") to demonstrate thorough, organic engagement with the discussion.
