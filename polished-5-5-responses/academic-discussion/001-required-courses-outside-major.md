# Required Courses Outside Major

## Prompt

**Professor's question:** In today's discussion, consider whether universities should require students to take courses outside their major (e.g., a science major taking an art class). Some say this broadens education; others say it wastes time that could be spent on the major itself. What is your view?

## My Draft

I think it is good idea for university require student take course outside their major. Because when student only study their major, they will miss many important knowledge in other field. For example, a science student who never learn about art or history maybe cannot understand the culture well. Also, in future job, sometimes you need knowledge from different area, not only your major. So I agree with require student take some course outside major, but not too many, maybe one or two class is enough.

## Polished Response (final — for review & teaching)

I believe universities should require students to take some courses outside their major, though the requirement should be limited in scope.

When students focus exclusively on their major, they often miss valuable knowledge in other fields. A science major who never studies art or history, for instance, may struggle to understand cultural or historical context that shapes real-world problems. Additionally, many careers today require knowledge that crosses disciplinary boundaries — a scientist who understands communication or ethics, for example, is often more effective in the workplace than one who does not.

That said, I would limit this requirement to one or two courses rather than a full curriculum, so students still have enough time to develop deep expertise in their chosen field.

## What Changed & Why

- **Missing article / subject-verb structure:** "it is good idea for university require student" → "it is a good idea for universities to require students" — articles (*a*, *the*) and infinitive constructions (*for X to do Y*) are required in English; omitting them sounds ungrammatical.
- **Sentence fragment ("Because…"):** Starting a sentence with *Because* without a main clause creates a fragment. The polished version integrates the reason smoothly into a full sentence (*"When students focus exclusively on their major, they often miss…"*).
- **Vague noun phrases:** "important knowledge in other field" → "valuable knowledge in other fields" — *valuable* is a stronger, more precise collocate than *important* here; *fields* must be plural when referring to the concept in general.
- **Weak example phrasing:** "maybe cannot understand the culture well" → "may struggle to understand cultural or historical context that shapes real-world problems" — adding concrete detail (*real-world problems*) strengthens the argument significantly.
- **Collocation — "future job":** In academic English, the conventional phrase is "careers" or "the workplace," not "future job" (which is informal and number-inconsistent).
- **Concession placement:** The draft buries the "not too many" qualifier at the end of one long sentence. The polished version gives it its own paragraph beginning with *"That said,"* — a clear concession signal that improves logical flow.

## Reusable Patterns

- **Scoped agreement opener:** "I believe [position], though the requirement should be limited in scope." — signals a nuanced stance immediately.
- **Exclusive-focus construction:** "When students focus exclusively on their [field/role], they often miss valuable knowledge in other areas." — adaptable to many discussion prompts about specialization vs. breadth.
- **Disciplinary-boundary argument:** "Many careers today require knowledge that crosses disciplinary boundaries." — broadly applicable to education and workforce discussions.
- **Comparative effectiveness closer:** "A [professional] who understands [Skill X], for example, is often more effective in the workplace than one who does not." — a tidy way to support a claim with a professional example.
- **Concession + limit clause:** "That said, I would limit this requirement to [X] rather than [Y], so students still have enough time to [goal]." — useful whenever you agree with an idea but want to set boundaries.
