# Universal Basic Income Debate

## Prompt
Your professor is teaching a class on economics. Write a post responding to the professor’s question.
In your response, you should do the following.
Express and support your opinion.
Make a contribution to the discussion in your own words.
An effective response will contain at least 100 words.
Dr. Gupta
We’ve been discussing the concept of universal basic income (UBI), where all citizens receive a regular, unconditional sum of money from the government. Some economists believe UBI can reduce poverty and stimulate economic growth, while others argue it may discourage people from working and contributing to society. What are your thoughts on implementing universal basic income? Would it be beneficial or harmful? Please explain.
Claire
I think UBI would be beneficial. It would provide financial security for everyone, especially the most vulnerable. It could help reduce poverty and give people the freedom to pursue education and entrepreneurial activities without the constant stress of financial instability.
Paul
In my opinion, UBI might be harmful. It could reduce the incentive for people to work and contribute to the economy. Additionally, funding such a program could require significant tax increases, which might negatively impact economic growth.

## My Polished Response
I agree with Paul’s analysis regarding the potential drawbacks of universal basic income, particularly concerning its negative impact on work incentives and the immense fiscal pressure it imposes on the state. While Claire is correct that UBI could open doors to education or entrepreneurial ventures for individuals who currently lack access, those benefits may be overshadowed by broader economic consequences. Guaranteed financial support without corresponding contributions risks lowering overall labor force participation and encouraging unproductive habits. First, when basic needs are met unconditionally, many individuals may choose to reduce their working hours or withdraw from the workforce entirely; a part-time retail worker who once needed thirty hours a week might drop to fifteen once the monthly transfer arrives, directly diminishing economic output and tax revenues. Second, funding such a massive welfare program requires substantial tax increases that could stifle innovation and slow long-term economic growth. Finally, without structured oversight, cash transfers do not guarantee that funds will be spent constructively. Therefore, while Claire identifies a real individual-level gain, Paul’s concerns about aggregate incentives and fiscal sustainability remain the more decisive factors, and implementing UBI would likely prove more harmful than beneficial to the broader economy.
