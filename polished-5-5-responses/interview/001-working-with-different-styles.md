# Working With Different Styles

## Prompt
Tell me about a time you had to work with someone whose working style was very different from yours. How did you handle it?

## My Draft
Yes I have this experience. In my last internship, I work with a colleague who like to do everything very fast, but I prefer to check detail carefully before submit. At first we have some conflict because he think I am too slow. But later I explain to him that checking detail can avoid mistake, and he explain to me why speed is important for the project deadline. So we find a way to combine, I check the important part carefully and he help speed up other simple part. Finally we finish project on time and no big mistake.

## Polished Response (final — for review & teaching)
Yes, I encountered this during my last internship. I worked with a colleague whose approach was much faster-paced than mine — he liked to move quickly through tasks, while I preferred to check details carefully before submitting anything.

At first, this created some friction; he felt I was moving too slowly, and I felt he was rushing important steps. To resolve this, we had an honest conversation: I explained that careful review helps us avoid costly mistakes, and he explained that speed was critical given our tight project deadline.

We ultimately agreed on a division of labor that respected both styles — I focused on carefully reviewing the most critical parts of the project, while he handled simpler tasks more quickly. In the end, we delivered the project on time without any major errors, and I learned that different working styles can actually complement each other when you communicate openly.

## What Changed & Why
- **Tense consistency:** The draft switches between present and past tense throughout ("I work," "he think," "we find"). English narration of past events requires consistent simple past ("I worked," "he thought," "we found").
- **Missing third-person -s:** Several verbs lacked the required -s for third-person singular (e.g., "he like to do," "he think"). Corrected to "he liked," "he felt," etc.
- **Vague nouns → precise collocations:** "check detail" became "check details carefully," and "no big mistake" became "without any major errors" — more natural, formal collocations for spoken academic English.
- **"Have some conflict" → "created some friction":** The draft's phrasing is grammatically awkward. "Created friction" is a natural, idiomatic collocation for describing interpersonal tension in a professional context.
- **"Find a way to combine" → "agreed on a division of labor":** The polished version uses a professional term ("division of labor") that signals workplace vocabulary and is more precise about what the compromise actually was.
- **Closing reflection added:** The draft ends abruptly with the outcome. The polished version adds a takeaway sentence ("I learned that different working styles can actually complement each other…"), which is expected in TOEFL interview answers to show depth of reflection.

## Reusable Patterns
- **Contrasting two styles:** "…whose approach was much [adjective]-er than mine — he liked to [X], while I preferred to [Y]." → Useful for any prompt comparing yourself to someone else.
- **Naming the turning point:** "To resolve this, we had an honest conversation:" → A clean, professional pivot phrase to introduce how a conflict was addressed.
- **Division of labor framing:** "We ultimately agreed on a division of labor that respected both styles — I focused on [X], while he handled [Y]." → Reusable whenever describing a collaborative compromise.
- **Outcome + lesson closing:** "In the end, we [achieved result], and I learned that [broader insight]." → A reliable two-part closing formula that satisfies the "how did it go / what did you learn" dimension of behavioral interview questions.
