## Title
The Reconstructive Nature of Memory

## TOEFL Domain
Cognitive Science

## Tier
T3 (D≈17)

## Core Thesis
Although people commonly view memory as a passive storage system that accurately records the past[cite: 2], and even though the natural deterioration of the prefrontal cortex impairs specific episodic recall as we age[cite: 2], memory actually functions as an active, reconstructive resource that shapes individual identity and collective beliefs to help humans navigate the present and anticipate the future[cite: 2].

## Pillar A
The widespread misconception that memory operates as a comprehensive archive leads to frustration over forgetting names or arbitrary details[cite: 2]; however, the brain inherently prioritizes emotionally salient or structurally meaningful information over random data[cite: 2], which causes everyday memories to be selective, reconstructive, and highly susceptible to subjective distortion[cite: 2].

## Pillar B
While semantic memory remains stable over a lifespan, the gradual functional decline of the prefrontal cortex after age thirty directly degrades the executive functions required to retrieve episodic memories[cite: 2], which explains why older adults struggle to recall specific contextual details despite fully retaining their accumulated general knowledge[cite: 2].

## Pillar C
Because memory is inherently malleable and heavily influenced by social reinforcement, the shared narratives constructed within communities not only forge strong collective identities[cite: 2], but they also create deep vulnerabilities to echo chambers and misinformation[cite: 2], ultimately demonstrating that our reconstructed pasts actively dictate our present societal frameworks[cite: 2].
