## Mental Model (read first)
ETS scores **communicative effectiveness under time pressure**, not polish. Every task rubric puts *elaboration* and *addressing the prompt* first, language accuracy last — and explicitly forgives "errors expected from a competent writer/speaker working under timed conditions." The single most consequential thing in these two chapters is the **inverse pair**: errors are cheap, and *memorized or formulaic language is expensive*. ETS states in four separate places that responses appearing to contain memorized text score considerably lower. So the exam rewards a slightly messy authentic voice over a clean rehearsed one.

## Writing Section (Chapter 4)

**Format**: ~12 questions, **linear** (everyone gets the same tasks regardless of prior performance). Three task types. For *Write an Email* and *Write for an Academic Discussion*, the scoring criteria explicitly **treat your response as a first draft** — a response with some errors can still earn the top score.

### Task 1 — Build a Sentence
- **Core idea**: Sentence-level grammar plus contextual appropriateness, measured jointly.
- **Mechanics**: A two-turn exchange. Turn 1 is complete; turn 2 has **3–7 blanks** plus jumbled words/phrases. Reorder them into turn 2. **~10 such questions per test.** Scored **right/wrong, no partial credit.**
- **Distractor rule** — some questions include **one extra word or phrase you must NOT use**. In the official examples the extras were *not* (in a negative question already carrying "Didn't") and *because* (where an embedded "why"-clause was required). **Tell**: the extra is usually a redundant negator or a conjunction that duplicates a function another word already performs.
- **How** (ETS's own strategy order): identify sentence type (question / statement / command) → locate subject + main verb → use turn 1 for setting, tone, topic → test arrangements against English syntax (SVO, adverb placement) → eliminate the extra → read it aloud (silently) for natural flow.
- **Anti-pattern** — solving for grammar only: a grammatical sentence that doesn't respond sensibly to turn 1 is still wrong. Both halves are scored.

### Task 2 — Write an Email
- **Core idea**: A **campus-life** scenario (situations on a university campus that don't involve academic content). The prompt always names a recipient and gives **three required actions** — e.g. *describe, explain, recommend* / *describe, explain, request*.
- **Measured**: meets the goal and follows normal social rules · explains ideas well and is easy to follow · correct grammar and vocabulary · spelling, punctuation, capitalization.
- **How**: read the prompt and *mentally list every required action* → plan 1–2 minutes (greeting, subject line, 3 ideas in logical order, 1–2 details each) → greeting, purpose, elaboration, closing request/question, sign-off → vary sentence types (statements, questions, conditionals) and include idiomatic phrases ("sounds good", "let me know") → attach concrete detail to each point → reserve ≥1 minute to check coverage and grammar.
- **Paragraphing**: not required. It's an email; connectedness and coherence are what's scored, not paragraph structure.
- **Length**: no target given by ETS. "You do not have to write a lengthy message" — a **concise message scores well if it is complete, relevant, and clearly organized.**
- **Anti-pattern** — restating the prompt: covering all three actions by paraphrasing the scenario back. It's coverage without elaboration and it reads as borrowed language.

### Task 3 — Write for an Academic Discussion
- **Core idea**: A professor poses a question; two students have posted. You add a post that **contributes to the discussion**.
- **Length**: **≥100 words** for an effective response — ETS states responses under 100 words typically can't demonstrate the development and variety needed for a **5**. **No maximum.** But "do not write just to be writing"; quality of ideas outranks number of ideas.
- **Contribution** — the load-bearing criterion, and narrower than it sounds:
  - You **do not have to respond** to the students' posts, but **you must not ignore them.**
  - If you use an idea a student already raised, **don't present it as if you're first to raise it.**
  - Agreeing is a legitimate contribution — *provided* you re-voice and extend it rather than repeat it.
  - Disagreeing, or introducing an entirely new relevant idea, are equally valid.
- **How**: read the professor's question *and* both posts → brainstorm briefly, then start writing (don't over-plan) → state your viewpoint clearly → use precise, not general, language → save time to proofread.
- **Anti-pattern** — copying phrasing from the other posts. Explicitly called out as not helping your score.

### Scoring Guides — Write an Email / Write for an Academic Discussion (both **0–5**)
Score **5 = "a fully successful response."** Four criteria, in ETS's own order:

| # | Email (score 5) | Academic Discussion (score 5) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Elaboration that **effectively supports the communicative purpose** | **Relevant and well-elaborated** explanations, exemplifications, and/or details |
| 2 | **Effective syntactic variety and precise, idiomatic word choice** | **Effective use of a variety of syntactic structures and precise, idiomatic word choice** |
| 3 | **Consistent use of appropriate social conventions** — politeness, register, organization of information, and *formulation of actions such as requests, refusals, criticisms* | *(no social-conventions criterion — it's a post, not a message to a person)* |
| 4 | Almost no lexical/grammatical errors **other than those expected from a competent writer writing under timed conditions** (common typos, there/their) | same |

Descending bands: **4** generally successful (adequate elaboration, few errors) · **3** partially successful (elaboration partially supports purpose; moderate range; noticeable errors) · **2** mostly unsuccessful (limited/irrelevant elaboration; accumulation of errors) · **1** unsuccessful (telegraphic language; minimal original language, mostly borrowed from the stimulus) · **0** blank, off-topic, not in English, entirely copied, or arbitrary keystrokes.

- **The language ceiling (decisive, stated for both tasks)**: *"If your language is hard to follow, your sentences are overly simple, and your vocabulary is limited, you may score no higher than a 3 — no matter how well you address the communication goals"* / *"no matter how impressive your ideas may be."* **Ideas cannot rescue language.** But note the converse is also enforced by criterion 1: language cannot rescue empty ideas.
- **Variety must be motivated**: "If you try to use varied structures or vocabulary without a good reason, that will not help you get a high score." Variety is scored as a *means of expressing precise ideas*, not as an ornament.

### The memorization penalty (ETS's most repeated warning)
Stated as a SMART TIP, in both task sections, and in the Speaking chapter. Three named failure modes, each illustrated in the guide:
1. **Long memorized introductions/conclusions** used to inflate word count ("The importance of the issue raised … cannot be underestimated…"). Called out as representing "neither authentic writing nor the type of writing used in online discussions."
2. **Memorized examples and formulaic source references** — the guide's specimen invents a *New York Times* poll with a fabricated statistic. Verdict: "not genuine development and will not be credited."
3. **Empty verbal padding** — many words asserting that an issue exists, developing no idea. A 100–200-word response made of this earns **1 or 2**.
- **Anti-pattern** — the "sophisticated" register trap: candidates reach for elevated diction ("a delicate line has to be trod", "transparently clear") believing it signals C2. ETS scores it as content-free. **Precision, not elevation.**

## Worked Example — the official score-5 email, and why the errors didn't matter
*(Reconstructed, not reproduced. Prompt: tell classmate Riley about a campus food truck — describe it, explain why Riley would like it, recommend a dish with reasons.)*

The rated 5 response runs ~110 words. Its moves: a one-line purpose statement ("I wanted to tell you about…") → the truck and its range, named concretely (India, Mexico, Thailand) with a price judgment → a *reason personalized to Riley* ("you always say you want to try new lunches") → a named dish with two sensory reasons → a forward-looking close offering a specific day.

It contains **three real errors**: a misformed plural, a subject–verb agreement failure, and a nonstandard spelling of *a lot*. ETS's rater commentary names them and scores the response a **5** anyway, because: all three prompt actions are elaborated; the ideas are logically connected; the sentence types vary; the idioms ("go together", "all sorts of") are natural; the tone is consistently polite; and *the errors never obscure meaning*.

**What this calibrates**: at score 5, the error budget is roughly "a few slips a fluent writer would make in seven minutes." What is *not* in budget is a generic response, a memorized frame, or an unaddressed bullet. Optimize in that order.

## Speaking Section (Chapter 5)

**Format**: ~11 questions, **linear**. **No preparation time on any task.** **Note-taking is NOT recommended** — it risks missing the beep and undercuts spontaneity. Every question involves listening first. Scored **holistically** (one score per response), 0–5.

**Three general scoring features** (all tasks): **Delivery** (clarity, pronunciation, pacing, natural intonation) · **Language Use** (grammar + vocabulary in service of ideas) · **Elaboration** (how clearly and thoroughly ideas are supported). Responses are *not expected to be perfect* — high-scoring spontaneous speech can contain occasional errors in any of the three.

### Task 1 — Listen and Repeat
- **Core idea**: Hear 7 sentences embedded in a realistic scenario (registrar's office, campus tour, recycling center) and repeat each **exactly once, immediately.**
- **Fixed progression** — memorize this shape:

| Block | Count | Syllables | Response time | Structure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Short | 2 | 9–11 | **8 s** | one independent clause, no modifiers |
| Medium | 3 | 14–16 | **10 s** | + prepositional phrases or a dependent clause |
| Long | 2 | 19–23 | **12 s** | multiple modifiers, serial lists, conditional + relative clauses |

- **Scored on two criteria only**: **Intelligibility** (how clearly you pronounce) and **Accuracy** (how closely you match). Nothing else — no ideas, no elaboration.
- **Score bands**: **5** = exact repetition, fully intelligible. **4** = meaning preserved, not exact — one or two function words changed/missing, one content word replaced by a related word, tense/number markers off, or two words transposed; self-correction allowed if the response completes. **3** = full sentence, majority of content words, but original meaning not accurately captured. **2** = large portion missing (typically the speaker repeats the front of the sentence then stalls); fragmentary. **1** = a few words, mostly unintelligible. **0** = nothing usable.
- **How**: use the on-screen image for context, but drop it if it distracts → **wait for the beep** (2-second pause after the audio) so the full response records → **finish the sentence even after a mistake** → repeat once only; leftover time is fine → **partial credit is awarded**, so always produce what you retained.
- **Anti-pattern** — stopping to fix a mispronunciation. It converts a likely 4 into a 2 by truncating the sentence. Push to the end.
- **Practice**: shadow sentences from audiobooks/video; the constraint is *syntactic retention*, so drill long conditional + relative-clause structures specifically.

### Task 2 — Take an Interview
- **Core idea**: A simulated conversation with a virtual interviewer in a realistic frame (scholarship application, research study). **4 questions, 45 seconds each, no prep time.** The interviewer nods and gestures while you speak.
- **Question progression** — early questions are personal/factual, later ones abstract and speculative:
  - **Personal**: past experience ("a time when your daily routine was disrupted") · plans/desires/goals ("career goals after you graduate") · preferences/things you're proud of · observations about people close to you ("how do your friends spend their weekends").
  - **Abstract/speculative**: opinion on a current topic or trend ("do students learn better in groups or individually — give reasons") · speculation about the future ("what will change about how people work in the next ten years") · **hypothetical** ("if you were to ask for help, would you ask a classmate or your professor — why").
- **Scored on four dimensions**: **Relevance** (addresses the question asked) · **Elaboration** (support) · **Delivery** (natural, fluent pace *including pauses and rhythm*) · **Language Use**.
- **Score 5** = on topic and **well elaborated**; good conversational pace with **appropriate and natural use of pauses**; pronunciation easily intelligible, rhythm and intonation convey meaning; range of accurate grammar and vocabulary expressing **precise** meanings.
  **4** = on topic and elaborated but may lack sentence-level connectors; some pausing minimally affecting flow. **3** = limited elaboration; **frequent or lengthy pauses producing a choppy pace, frequent fill words**. **2** = minimally connected, little relevant elaboration, mostly recycled language from the question. **1** = vaguely connected, mostly unintelligible, isolated words.
- **How**: use the scenario introduction to start retrieving relevant experiences *before* Q1 → answer the actual question directly → elaborate with a brief story, a reason, or your reasoning process → keep a steady pace; some "um"/"you know" is normal, long gaps are not → **use the full 45 seconds**; if you finish early, add a detail or example rather than repeating → imagine talking to a friend.
- **Anti-pattern** — memorized openers/closers to pad length. Explicitly named in this chapter's strategy list.
- **Accent**: "A natural accent is expected and does not affect your score unless it interferes with intelligibility."
- **No re-recording**: you may self-correct mid-response, but you cannot redo an answer.

### Calibration from the four official score-5 interview samples
All four rated-5 transcripts are conspicuously **unpolished spoken English**: they open with "Um… I think it depends", "Let me think… um", "Uh, yeah"; they contain mid-sentence restarts ("I was… I mean, I was feeling"), hedges ("like", "I guess"), and one even concedes incompetence ("I guess I am not very good at making quick decisions for important things"). ETS's commentary calls these "minor slips typical of a **C1-level speaker**" and awards full marks. What earns the 5 in every case is the same trio: a **directly responsive stance**, a **concrete personal example** (the omelet, the classmate who knows phones, the course chosen after asking classmates), and **an explicit contrast that shows range** (small decisions vs. big ones; deciding alone vs. consulting).

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- **Optimize in rubric order, not in nervousness order**: (1) address every required action / the actual question, (2) elaborate concretely, (3) vary syntax *for a reason*, (4) accuracy last. Time spent hunting a rarer word is misspent if a bullet is thin.
- **When a phrase feels impressive, delete it.** ETS's two negative specimens are both elevated-register padding. If a sentence would survive being moved to a different prompt unchanged, it is formulaic — rewrite it around the specific scenario.
- **When you notice an error mid-response**: writing → fix it in the proofread pass, not now. Speaking → keep going to the end of the sentence. Truncation costs more than the error.
- **When the Discussion prompt's two students have both said something you agree with**: agree explicitly with one *by name*, then add a mechanism or consequence they didn't state. Repetition scores nothing; extension scores as contribution.
- **When you're under 100 words on the Discussion task**, you are structurally capped below a 5 — add a *second dimension* of the argument (cost, age, quality, timing), not more adjectives.
- **When you finish an Interview answer early**, add a contrast or a second example. Never restate.
- **When an idea is impressive but your sentence is muddy**, simplify the sentence. The score-3 ceiling for unclear language is absolute; there is no ideas-based override.
- **Default error budget**: a handful of slips that never obscure meaning is *inside* the score-5 band on every rubric here. Stop trying to be perfect; start trying to be specific.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Elaboration + relevance are criterion #1 on every scored task**; accuracy is last and explicitly forgiven within limits.
2. **Memorized/formulaic language is the one thing scored punitively** — it is named in the Writing tips, both writing rubrics' commentary, and the Speaking strategies.
3. **A hard 3-ceiling** applies to writing that is hard to follow, oversimple, or lexically thin, regardless of content quality.
4. **Speaking has no prep time and note-taking is discouraged** — fluency must be built, not staged.
5. *Listen and Repeat* is a **retention-and-intelligibility** test with a fixed 2/3/2 syllable ladder (8/10/12 s) and **partial credit**: always finish the sentence.
6. *Take an Interview* is 4 × 45 s, progressing personal → abstract/speculative; the winning shape is **stance + concrete personal example + a contrast**.
7. **The official top-scoring samples contain visible errors and audible fillers.** Calibrate to them, not to an imagined flawless answer.
